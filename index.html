Novi Connect · Q4 2025 · Designed, built, evaluated

Product categorization a major tech platform could accept

Product data from about fifty web scrapes and CSV files had to be normalized and categorized before it went to a major tech platform. Categories had to be consistent across products, and plenty of edge cases needed a person.

Normalize→Rules→Embeddings→LLM→Human review↺Corrections feed back
  • Used deterministic rules where they were enough, and embeddings built from a labeled golden dataset to keep categories consistent.
  • Sent only the uncertain cases to an LLM, and the hardest ones to a person whose corrections improve later assignments.
  • Tested against labeled products with their categories stripped, then added an LLM judge to catch drift once labeled data ran out.

My role: gathered the data, designed the architecture, built the tool in Lovable, and designed and ran the evals.

Eval resultsBoth bars met
99%accuracy on categories the system assigned on its own
80%+of products categorized with no human touch
~50messy source files reconciled into one format

Novi Connect · 2025 · Defined the quality bar

An LLM document validator where a miss costs more than a false flag

Brands submit documents to back the claims they make about their products, and Target's team reviewed every one by hand for its Target Zero program. A wrongly approved document is far more costly than one a person has to clear.

  • Kept the policy logic deterministic and gave the model only narrow checks, such as whether a required label appears.
  • Made recall on invalid documents the primary metric, because that is the expensive mistake.
  • Shipped with a human override on every decision and audit reporting.

My role: framed the problem, chose the metric, and set the bar. An engineering colleague ran the evaluation against 223 human-labeled documents.

Eval resultsShipped
100%recall on invalid documents
−60%false rejections (accuracy 53% → 81%)
5h → 30mreview time per batch
~$0.01per document

Side project · Fall 2026 · In progress

Is my grocery store using dynamic pricing?

King Soopers now uses electronic shelf labels, which can change prices at any time. I designed a study to find out whether, where, and how often one store actually uses that: 57 items, checked at the same time every day for three weeks, so each weekday appears three times.

  • Grouped items by how they're likely priced instead of by aisle: commodity perishables, markdown items, staples shoppers price-check, promo-driven center store, private-label vs. national-brand pairs, and obscure long-tail items as a control. If the bay leaves move, something automated is running.
  • Planned around the biggest confound, the weekly ad cycle: record promo status on every tag and analyze regular prices separately. Started after Labor Day so the holiday wouldn't distort the window.
  • Early finding from scouting: the app's "in store" price didn't consistently match the shelf tag. The app couldn't replace store visits, so the gap became a variable to measure.

Next: deciding what to build on the data.

How I work

The same four habits show up in every project above.

Fix the data first

Most product problems, and most AI quality problems, are data problems. Clean integrations, ontologies, and golden datasets come before clever features.

Decide what the model decides

Policy stays deterministic where it can. The model gets narrow, checkable jobs, and a person can always step in.

Measure the expensive mistake

Pick the metric that matches what failure costs, set the bar before building, and test against labeled data.

Build it to learn it

I prototype and ship with Claude Code alongside engineering, so decisions come from working software instead of slides.
